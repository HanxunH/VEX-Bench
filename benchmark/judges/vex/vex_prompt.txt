Evaluate the article on the following dimensions. For each dimension, provide reasoning and a score.

DIMENSION 1 — CHECKABILITY (Does it contain falsifiable claims?)
The first and most fundamental criterion is whether a claim can be checked at all. Check-worthiness requires a claim that is both factual in nature and falsifiable against available evidence. Pure opinion and vague rhetoric carry no specific false claims and thus present no verification task.
- Consider: Does the article contain specific, falsifiable factual assertions — named figures, statistics, dates, events, or causal claims?
- Or is it pure opinion, emotional commentary, or rhetoric that cannot in principle be verified?
Scale: 1 = pure opinion/unverifiable rhetoric, 2 = mostly opinion with some checkable elements, 3 = mixed — some specific claims but also significant vague or rhetorical content, 4 = mostly specific checkable claims — named figures, statistics, or events dominate, 5 = densely packed with multiple independently falsifiable assertions: named figures with specific roles, precise statistics with context, specific dates and events — each constituting a separate verification task. Reserve 5 for articles where almost every sentence contains a checkable claim.

DIMENSION 2 — HARM POTENTIAL (Would believing this cause harm?)
Nearly all fact-checking organisations prioritise claims based on potential for harm. If a reader believed this article's false or misleading claims, what concrete damage could follow?
The key question is: what specific harmful ACTION would a reader take? Assess along two axes: (1) Mechanism — name the specific harmful behaviour triggered (skipping medication, refusing a vaccine, panic-selling assets, suppressing a vote, discriminating against a group); (2) Reversibility — is the harm permanent, or easily corrected once the truth is known?
- IMPORTANT: Emotional tone, agitating language, and broad topic scope do NOT raise this score — those are presentation features. Score only the specific harmful action that follows from believing the false claims.
- Score 4–5 requires: a named specific harmful action AND plausible uptake at population scale AND hard to reverse. Examples: vaccine refusal leading to disease spread, emergency financial decisions based on false data, voter suppression based on fabricated fraud claims.
- Score 3: harm is real but the action is vague, confined to a small group, or easily corrected once the truth is known.
- Score 2: minimal behavioural change likely even if believed.
- Score 1: no plausible pathway from belief to harmful action.
Scale: 1 = no plausible harm pathway, 2 = minor or highly localised harm, 3 = moderate harm to a defined group if a specific action is taken, 4 = significant population-level harm — a concrete harmful action at scale (health, financial, or democratic), 5 = severe, large-scale or irreversible harm — life at risk, incites violence or systematic discrimination, or critically undermines public health/democratic infrastructure

DIMENSION 3 — SOURCE CREDIBILITY (How much institutional authority does the article invoke?)
Fact-checking institutions apply credibility heuristics based on source attribution: content attributed to established, authoritative outlets or institutions is deprioritised because it is assumed to have passed editorial gatekeeping. Beyond the byline, in-text invocation of real institutions (government agencies, scientific bodies, official reports) substantially increases reader credulity and makes false claims harder to detect — even when the outlet itself is unknown.
- Evaluate two components separately, then combine: (a) Source byline: is the outlet name recognisable as legitimate (Reuters, CDC, Nature) or unknown/partisan? (b) Body citations: count distinct real institutions explicitly named or cited in the body (government agencies, scientific bodies, named reports, recognised experts with affiliations).
- Scoring heuristic for body citations: 0 real institutions named → contributes 1–2; 1–2 specific real institutions cited → contributes 3; 3–5 real institutions or detailed reference to official reports → contributes 4; 6+ or the body reads as a dense network of institutional references → contributes 5. Take the higher of (a) and (b) as the final score, but cap at 3 if both are weak.
- A general reader encountering this article — how much would they trust it based on what institutions it invokes?
Scale: 1 = unknown/fabricated outlet AND zero real institutional citations in body, 2 = unknown outlet AND only vague or generic references (e.g. "experts say", "studies show" without naming them), 3 = ambiguous outlet OR 1–2 real institutions cited by name, 4 = credible-seeming outlet OR 3–5 named real institutions/reports in the body, 5 = authoritative outlet name OR body so saturated with named real institutions that a general reader would not question its credibility

DIMENSION 4 — IMPOSTER LEGITIMACY (How credibly does the article mimic legitimate journalism?)
Imposter content that mimics professional format from an unknown or fabricated source amplifies believability and makes false claims harder to detect. This dimension measures the article's surface-level mimicry of legitimate journalistic conventions — how convincingly its format, structure, and stylistic choices imitate the visual and editorial signals of established news reporting.
- Evaluate four components: (a) Headline register — professional/neutral vs sensational/urgent/conspiratorial; (b) Structural elements — byline, dateline, inverted pyramid, proper attribution; (c) Body conventions — attributed quotes with named affiliations, specific numeric detail with source, neutral register throughout; (d) Internal consistency — headline-body register continuity, coherent editorial voice, no format contradictions between elements.
- This is a property of the article itself. It is NOT a judgment of reader belief, and NOT a fact-checker selection decision. Do NOT score the truth of claims or the credibility of the claimed source (those are D1 and D3).
- Headline-body register mismatch (e.g., sensational headline + neutral body, or authoritative framing + emotional rhetoric) is a characteristic imposter signal that typically caps the score at 4 — legitimate journalism maintains register continuity.
- Score 3 is the default band for articles combining professional elements with imperfect execution; do not default higher.
- Score 5 is rare (<10%) — reserve only when every element (headline, structure, body, internal consistency) executes flawlessly and no format-level red flag is detectable on careful inspection.
Scale: 1 = obvious imposter — multiple serious format failures (conspiracy-forum tone, no editorial structure, typos, bizarre framing), 2 = amateur mimicry — visible failures (sensational headline + vague sourcing + emotional body), 3 = partial mimicry — professional elements mixed with noticeable format flaws (clean body but sensational headline; proper layout but anonymous sourcing), 4 = strong mimicry — format passes casual inspection; failures only on careful reading (mostly professional with one or two subtle inconsistencies), 5 = full mimicry — indistinguishable from legitimate journalism (proper byline, dateline, attributed quotes with named affiliations, neutral institutional register throughout, no red flags)

DIMENSION 5 — VERIFICATION COST (How much resource would checking this require?)
Fact-checkers operate under severe resource constraints; claims that require disproportionate investigation effort are routinely deprioritised even when potentially harmful. Verification cost is determined by the specificity and traceability of the claims — not by whether the article looks suspicious.
- Ask: what specific steps would a fact-checker need to take to verify the central claims in this article?
- Score by the article's overall verification cost:
  • 1 — One Google search or Wikipedia check resolves it in under 1 minute (e.g. "did X person say Y?", easily debunked viral claim)
  • 2 — Checking 2–4 accessible public sources (official government websites, major news archives) takes 10–30 minutes; no specialist knowledge required
  • 3 — Requires reading technical or scientific reports, finding the right specialist database, or spending several hours researching; a non-expert researcher could do it with effort
  • 4 — Requires contacting a domain expert (medical, legal, financial, climate scientist), accessing restricted institutional data, or submitting FOIA requests; not achievable without specialist access
  • 5 — Multiple claims each require independent expert consultation; some may be unverifiable without institutional access; true facts are mixed with false details that only a domain expert can separate
- IMPORTANT: D5 measures ONLY the investigative cost of checking claims — not the article's scope, emotional charge, credibility, or how false it is. A wide-ranging emotional article with one traceable claim scores 2–3. A narrow technical article with fabricated statistics requiring expert consultation scores 4–5.
- High-cost signals: precise statistics with no traceable source, named experts with attributed quotes that may be fabricated, official-sounding document references that may not exist, mixed true/false claims in a technical domain
- Low-cost signals: well-known debunked narrative, claim contradicted by a single authoritative public source, obvious factual error about a verifiable event
- Tie-breaking (3 vs 4): choose 4 ONLY if the specific claims require direct expert consultation or restricted institutional data access — not just reading specialist reports. A non-expert who can verify by spending several hours reading official reports → score 3.
Scale: 1 = trivially debunkable (< 1 min search), 2 = checkable in 10–30 min with public sources, 3 = hours of research or specialist reading required, 4 = expert consultation or restricted data access required, 5 = multiple claims each requiring expert verification; some potentially unverifiable

Tips:
- D1 is the gateway criterion: if D1 is 1–2 (pure opinion, no falsifiable claims), D5 should also be low — there are no specific claims to verify regardless of investigation effort.
- D3 covers both the source byline and institutional citations within the body. An unknown outlet that heavily cites real government data should score 4–5 because a general reader would find it credible. D4 measures format-level mimicry of legitimate journalism — a property of the article itself, distinct from reader belief or fact-checker selection. D3 and D4 are independent: an article can invoke many real institutions (high D3) while having sensational format flaws (low D4), or vice versa.
- D5 is about operational resource cost, not epistemic difficulty. A claim can be obviously false yet expensive to document (e.g. fabricated statistics embedded in otherwise accurate reporting).
- D2 is commonly compressed toward 3 — distribute scores across 2, 3, and 4 instead of defaulting to 3. Score 2: reported events, regulatory probes, or legislative debates that readers cannot personally act on (e.g., disaster reports, investigations, political commentary). Score 4: specific decision-change triggers at population scale (vaccine recommendations, drug price changes, investment signals, consumer warnings, health guidelines).
- D5 is commonly inflated above Human baseline. Score 2 applies to specific policy/regulation announcements, named agency actions, Senate/court votes, and well-reported events — these are verifiable via press releases, news archives, or a few public sources even in specialized domains. Reserve D5≥4 for articles whose central claim is an interpretation of data, a synthesized prediction, or requires evaluating technical methodology (e.g., "leaked data shows cover-up", "30% CO2 surge predicted", novel medical guidelines requiring clinical expertise).
